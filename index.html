APP STORE OPTIMIZATION · ORGANIC DISCOVERY

Making OneTap easier to find.

Six months of keyword testing and creator outreach helped OneTap grow to nearly 3,000 monthly downloads from App Store Search and Browse, without paid ads.

My role
Founder, product designer, developer, and marketer
Product
OneTap: Clipboard Manager
2,916Downloads from App Store search and browse
October 2026 · 2,708 from search + 208 from browse

The problem

OneTap was getting roughly 10–20 downloads a day. I had built a useful clipboard and keyboard product, but I needed to make it easier for people looking for those tools to find it.

What I changed

Over more than six months, I tested keywords and app names around clipboard management and copying and pasting. I moved “Clipboard Manager” to the front of the app name to make OneTap’s purpose clear at a glance.

I initially tested changes without promotion, looking for roughly 5–25 additional daily downloads before bringing more attention to the app. I then worked with creators on YouTube and TikTok features and watched what happened after each feature.

What happened

Over that period, daily downloads grew from roughly 10–20 to 80–120. I looked beyond the initial spike: after creator features, downloads often stayed higher for weeks. That gave me a reason to keep refining the listing and the keywords behind it.

October’s acquisition report recorded 3,128 total downloads. App Store search accounted for 2,708 and browse for 208—a combined 2,916. The remaining downloads came from app referrals, web referrals, and unknown sources.

What I learned

The App Store listing is part of the product experience. Naming OneTap around the job people wanted to do made its value easier to understand. Testing before promotion also gave me a clearer baseline for deciding what to try next.

I treated metadata and the product page as different parts of the same experience: helping people find OneTap, then helping them understand it. Apple’s search guidance ↗

August was the highest complete month in the November 2025–October 2026 report reviewed. These are dated usage results. Onboarding and acquisition both changed over time, so the chart does not isolate the effect of the setup redesign.

The problem

Creator features were bringing spikes in installs, but shortcut usage was not growing with them. My historical estimate for January 2024 to January 2025 is roughly 1,000–5,000 shortcut uses per month. I suspected people were getting stuck before they could use the product: enabling the OneTap keyboard required a trip into iOS Settings.

What I changed

I researched how other keyboard apps handled setup and focused on the handoff between the app and Settings. The flow needed to show people what to do and keep that guidance available while they did it.

I added a “Set Up Keyboard” action to onboarding that opened the keyboard settings. I paired it with a floating video guide using Picture in Picture, so the demonstration could remain visible while users completed the steps in Settings.

What happened

After I introduced the setup action and floating guide, monthly shortcut usage passed 30,000 and later reached 50,000. Compared with roughly 5,000 uses a month before the redesign, that was a tenfold increase, or 900% growth. These are historical estimates, rather than a controlled experiment.

The jump in shortcut usage suggested that more people were getting through keyboard setup successfully and using OneTap. It reinforced my belief that the missing step was activation: people needed help enabling the keyboard before they could experience the product’s value.

The later Mixpanel data gives a clearer picture of scale: recorded keyboard shortcut usage rose from 58,082 in January to 289,157 in August 2026, nearly five times January’s volume. In August, 1,984 unique users used the keyboard and 1,202 unique users created a shortcut.

Helping people get the keyboard working was just as important as getting them to download the app. I looked at these usage milestones alongside changes in acquisition, rather than treating all growth as the result of one design change.

What I learned

The setup experience continued outside my app. Designing that transition meant keeping instructions available at the moment people needed them, without asking them to remember a sequence of steps while switching screens.

PRODUCT DESIGN · PRICING & MONETIZATION

Finding a pricing model that fits the product.

A hard paywall increased active trials by 324%. Cancellations and continued lifetime purchases told a more useful story about what customers valued.

My role
Founder, product designer, and developer
Product
OneTap: Clipboard Manager
+324%Increase in active trials over 25 days
25 on September 14 → peak of 106 on October 9, 2026
September 1425
October 9 · peak106

Two active-trial snapshots from the original analytics: +81, or +324%, over 25 days. This is not a trial-start count or a paid-conversion rate.

The problem

OneTap was generating around $2,000 a month, driven by lifetime purchases, while monthly recurring revenue was around $200. I wanted to understand whether subscriptions could become a stronger part of the business. The optional paywall was not producing enough trial activity to give me a clear answer.

What I changed

I introduced a hard paywall during onboarding, offering a subscription trial alongside a discounted lifetime purchase. I also tested regional pricing to see whether a better fit with local markets would improve the outcome.

What happened

The active-trial count rose from 25 on September 14 to a peak of 106 on October 9. That was an increase of 81 active trials, or 324%, over 25 days.

I also saw more than 75% of trials canceled. Regional pricing changes did not resolve that pattern, and lifetime purchases continued. The larger active-trial count gave me more activity to examine, but it was not enough to show that the subscription model was working.

The next iteration

On November 1, I removed the hard paywall. I restored an optional discounted lifetime offer immediately after onboarding, available only at that moment, so people could continue into the product without committing to a purchase.

As of November 10, revenue was on pace to exceed October. That was an early projection; November’s final revenue and the longer-term effect on recurring revenue were still unknown.

What I learned

A higher trial count was not the outcome I was really after. I needed to understand whether people wanted an ongoing subscription or a useful tool they could buy once. Looking at cancellations, lifetime purchases, and revenue together helped me make a better product decision.
